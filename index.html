How faint a sign of vulnerability can a language model detect?

A customer asking about their pension mentions, in passing, that their husband used to handle the money. Sounding measures how subtle a remark like that can be before a model notices it and changes how it responds, by treating the model as the observer in a psychophysics experiment.

Status: simulation only. No real model has been evaluated yet and nothing on this page is a result. The instrument below runs on simulated observers whose behaviour is set by the sliders.

Two ways to look more careful

Suppose a firm adds a line to its system prompt: take extra care with vulnerable customers. Afterwards the model flags more of them. That could mean it has become better at seeing faint cues. It could also mean it has simply become more willing to say “vulnerable”, including to customers who gave no sign of it. A count of flagged customers cannot tell these apart. The psychometric function can, because the two changes move different parts of the curve.

What you are looking at

The horizontal axis is cue intensity: how strongly a remark signals vulnerable circumstances, from the faintest at 1 to explicit at 4. The vertical axis is the probability that the observer detects it. The curve is the psychometric function:

P(detect | cue at intensity x) = γ + (1 − γ − λ) · logistic((x − α) / β)
P(detect | no cue) = γ

The dashed brown curve is the baseline observer and never moves. The teal curve is the observer set by the controls. The red line marks its threshold. Open circles are a simulated experiment: at each intensity the observer is shown n messages, and the circle is the share it detected, with a bar of one standard error either side. The leftmost circle is the catch trials, messages with no cue at all.

Threshold α
The intensity at which detection is halfway up the curve. Lower is better: the observer picks up fainter cues. This is the quantity the project exists to measure.
False-alarm rate γ
How often the observer reports vulnerability when there is no cue. It sets the floor of the curve. Raising it lifts every point, which looks like improvement if you only count detections.
Slope β
How sharply detection rises around the threshold. A small value means a crisp boundary between missed and noticed.
Lapse rate λ
How often even an unmissable cue is missed. It sets the ceiling.
Trials per point n
This changes nothing about the observer. It changes how precisely the circles pin down the curve, which is what a power analysis decides.

The four readouts summarise the teal observer, with the change from baseline beneath each. Hit rate is the detection probability averaged over the four cue intensities. False alarms is γ. Sensitivity d′ is the distance between the two after converting each to a z-score, and criterion c is minus their average: negative c means an observer inclined to say “vulnerable” either way.

Press More perceptive and the threshold falls while the floor stays put, so d′ rises. Press More cautious and the floor rises while the threshold stays put: more detections, but d′ falls, because the extra detections are bought with false alarms.

What is idealised

Everything here is a simulation. Real models may not follow a logistic curve, and finding out is part of the work. The 1 to 4 intensity scale is provisional; in the experiment it is replaced by a scale built from human pairwise judgements of which remark signals more. The d′ and c shown use the equal-variance signal detection model on a hit rate pooled across intensities, which is a coarse summary. The analysis itself fits the whole curve with a hierarchical Bayesian model.

The four questions

  1. Threshold. How subtle a cue can each model detect, for each of the four drivers of vulnerability in the Financial Conduct Authority’s guidance: health, life events, resilience and capability?
  2. Sensitivity or caution. Does a system prompt asking for care lower the threshold, or only raise the false-alarm rate?
  3. Knowing versus doing. Does a model that spots a cue when asked outright also adapt its reply when nobody asks?
  4. Temporary versus enduring. Are temporary circumstances harder to detect than lasting ones?

Synthetic and public data only

Every customer message in the project is invented for it and describes no real person. There is no customer data and no employer data. Categories and definitions come from published regulatory guidance. The rule is enforced in code: each stimulus carries a record of where it came from, a guard rejects anything that is not synthetic or that resembles personal data, and the check runs on every change to the repository.
